\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

In an Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN), applications from different vendors, known as xApps, run on the near-real-time
RAN Intelligent Controller (near-RT RIC) and adjust radio control parameters to pursue different optimisation
objectives. These objectives can conflict even when the xApps do not write the same parameter. For example, an
energy-saving xApp may request that a lightly loaded pico cell enter sleep mode. Traffic previously served by that cell
must then be handled by neighbouring cells, potentially increasing their load and degrading the service experienced by
users of a protected network slice. A slice-protection xApp may respond by adjusting resource allocation, but its
actions may not eliminate the degradation introduced by the sleep decision. Thus, a locally beneficial action can have
adverse effects elsewhere in the network. O-RAN Working Group~3 distinguishes direct conflicts, in which xApps write
the same parameter; indirect conflicts, in which different parameters affect a common key performance indicator
(KPI); and implicit conflicts, in which adverse interactions arise through the network without a shared
parameter~\cite{ORANConflictMit} (Figure~\ref{fig:intro_conflict}). Detecting and resolving indirect and implicit
conflicts requires more than identifying which xApps issued requests: it requires evidence about how control actions
affect network performance.

Recent work identifies parameter--KPI relationships using learned conflict graphs, sandbox profiles and SHAP-informed
causal models~\cite{santos2026conflictairan,PACIFISTA,Sharma_2025}. However, establishing causal effects from
operational logs remains difficult because xApps act in response to network state. For example, an energy-saving xApp may request cell
sleep precisely when traffic is low, while the subsequent load and service quality of neighbouring cells depend on
traffic conditions that also influence the observed KPIs. Comparing periods with and without sleep requests can
therefore mix the effect of the action with the conditions that prompted it. We investigate a designed-logging
approach in which the near-RT RIC's conflict-mitigation layer randomly accepts or rejects xApp requests and records the
assignment probabilities~\cite{ORANRICARCH}. This creates controlled variation in which requests are applied and
supports design-based causal testing without requiring a model of the network. The resulting tests assess whether
parameter families affect KPIs under the specified randomisation design; however, discovering effects does not by
itself establish their signs, localise them to particular cells, or show that a policy based on the resulting map will
improve performance safely.

We evaluate this approach on three questions: whether a design-based test is valid on such logs, whether the causal
map it learns agrees with an independent ground truth, and whether decisions taken with the map improve on static
rules within the safety limits. The evaluation uses a system-level simulation of an O-RAN surge scenario, in which
energy-saving xApps conflict with a slice-protection xApp, and controlled environments with known causal structure.

The first two answers are positive. The test declared no effect on placebo logs and never reported an effect with the
wrong sign, and most of the effects it reported were confirmed by a knockout ground truth, on uniform-randomised and
state-dependent logs alike. In the controlled environments, tests that ignored the logging design lost their error control
once the logging followed a dithered setpoint instead of independent draws, whereas tests that used the design kept
it. The third answer is negative: a referee acting on the map reduced protected-slice violations but breached a
radio-link-failure limit, and behind a gate that admits only certified-safe rejections it fell back to the best static
rule, as did the true causal map. In this scenario, the safety guards rather than knowledge of the network limit what
conflict mitigation can achieve.

The paper makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The test}. PMRT learns signed causal effect maps from request logs with recorded assignment
probabilities, with an explicit estimand and conditions for asymptotic validity.
\item \textbf{The validity evidence}. Simulator null checks and five controlled environments test whether discovery
methods retain their error rate under known logging designs, and show that passing a placebo check is necessary but
not sufficient.
\item \textbf{The decision study}. A map-driven referee and edge-certification gate test whether a valid map improves
safe decisions; in the evaluated scenario it does not, which is the paper's central result.
\end{enumerate}
The work replaces, rather than extends, the method of our conference paper CDD O-RAN~\cite{dahal2026cddoran}.
